# Baked static GI and lightmaps: how noise-free systems are built, and how harbagsa compares

**Question:** How are noise-free baked static global-illumination and lightmap systems built, and how does harbagsa's static bake compare?

**Date:** 2026-11-10

**Status:** Complete. Research snapshot; no code or build changes. Sources were checked on 2026-11-10. Claims that could not be verified are listed under **Not found** rather than guessed.

**Scope:** "Noise-free" here means no random variance: deterministic sampling, no stochastic noise, no temporal denoiser. Deterministic quadrature error and discretization error still count as error. The existing offline bake and the existing hard ray-query direct shadows are the baseline. Real-time path-traced/ray-traced GI systems appear only as shipped-engine context; the offline bake's existing deterministic ray sampling is part of the baseline, not a new recommendation.

## Answer

Radiosity is the noise-free reference. Goral et al. (1984) showed that diffuse interreflection can be precomputed by solving a linear system of form factors between surface patches; the result is view-independent, includes colour bleeding, and has no random sampling at all. The hemi-cube made the form factors practical for complex scenes. Progressive refinement reduced memory to O(n) and produced a usable image in time linear in the patch count. Hierarchical and wavelet radiosity reduced the form-factor matrix from O(n²) to O(n) links/time. The error in this family is discretization error: patch size, visibility resolution, and the basis used to represent the radiance function. It is not sampling noise. This is the family harbagsa's per-texel gather belongs to, even though harbagsa does not build a patch matrix.

Shipped engines split into two families. Quake 3's q3map2 and Valve's VRAD are deterministic radiosity bakers. q3map2 re-emits surface light as diffuse lights and re-lights the map per bounce. VRAD does patch-based diffuse radiosity with a bounce cap, adaptive supersampling, and an early-out. Source's VRAD bakes direct and indirect into per-face lightmaps, uses RGBE for HDR, and stores three extra lightmaps for radiosity normal mapping on bump-lit faces. Enlighten is also radiosity-based, but its precompute samples visibility with rays; its runtime solve is deterministic given the form factors. The second family is stochastic: Unreal Lightmass (photons plus sampling), Unity's Progressive Lightmapper (path samples plus denoisers), Frostbite's Flux (path-traced SH lightmaps), and idTech 7's path-traced lightmaps. Those systems are useful evidence for budgets and storage, not for a noise-free bake.

The production numbers set the scale. Frostbite targeted 20-25 cm per texel (4-5 texels/m) for *Star Wars Battlefront II*. idTech 7's baked lightmaps defaulted to 2x2 texels per square metre (2 texels per linear metre, 50 cm per texel) and still needed 4-10 hours for a final quality bake. Source's default is 16 Hammer units per luxel, roughly 30-41 cm depending on the unit convention; q3map2's default is also 16 units. Unity's Progressive Lightmapper defaults to 40 texels per unit, which is much finer, but that is an editor default, not a shipped-game density. harbagsa's 4 texels/m (25 cm) sits at the production-reasonable end, not the coarse end.

The atlas is a different story. harbagsa's 1024² atlas has 1,048,576 texels; 165,334 valid texels is 15.8% of the atlas, but that is not packing efficiency. The 4-texel gutter and 8-texel minimum chart dominate small faces: a 1 m face at 4 texels/m has 4x4 = 16 useful texels but is forced to 8x8 = 64 valid texels inside a 16x16 = 256-texel allocation. That is 25% chart efficiency. At 25-44% efficiency, the current content occupies roughly 0.4-0.7M of the 1.05M atlas texels, i.e. the atlas is about 35-63% allocated. Reduce the gutter to 1 texel and the minimum chart to 4, and the same content would use several times less atlas. That is the cheapest growth path, before a larger atlas or a compressed format.

The fixed atlas has three limits, in order. First is padding: with the current 4-texel gutter and 8-texel minimum, a 2x linear arena (4x surface) would need roughly 1.6-2.6M atlas texels and would not fit. With 96% packing (Frostbite's global packer reports 96% on 263k charts), the same content would need about 689k texels and would fit. Second is the 32 MiB artifact budget: 2048² RGBA16F is exactly 32 MiB (33.55 MB) for the lightmap alone, so it consumes the whole budget before the probes and is over once probes and headers are added. Two 1024² pages, or a 4-byte HDR encoding such as RGBE, fit; BC6H is 8 bpp and fits easily but needs format support. Third is bake time: the current 165,334 texels x 128 rays = 21.2M rays for the lightmap stage, about 3.0 s; a second per-texel bounce doubles that. A patch-level radiosity solve can add bounces more cheaply than a per-texel gather, but it is a larger implementation.

What is under-engineered: one bounce (no second-order colour bleeding), a fixed 3x3 binomial that softens contact corners by one texel (25 cm), a single atlas page, and no maximum chart size/subdivision for very large faces. What is over-engineered: the 4-texel gutter (one texel is enough for a bilinear tap), the 8-texel minimum chart (4 is enough for a 3x3 chart-local filter), and RGBA16F at 1024² (fine now, but it blocks a single 2048² atlas once the probes are included). The probe stage is separately expensive for its role: 1024 rays per probe for a term only dynamic boxes sample. The 2 s bake target is missed by about 2.5x; the lightmap stage alone is about 3.0 s, and production bakes are minutes to hours. The 5 s bake is not the quality problem. The quality knobs are the second bounce and the filter; the capacity knobs are the gutter, the minimum chart, and the atlas format.

A noise-free multi-bounce bake can be built two ways. The smaller change is a deterministic lightmap-feedback gather: keep the current 128-ray first-bounce pass, store it, then trace another 128 rays per texel and sample the first-bounce lightmap at each hit; repeat for bounce 3. That is deterministic, uses the existing BVH, and costs about one lightmap stage per bounce. The larger change is patch-level progressive/hierarchical radiosity: one patch per face or a quadtree, form factors by hemi-cube/rays, sorted shooting to convergence, then interpolate to lightmap texels. That is the classical noise-free method and can be cheaper per bounce, but it needs a patch solver, discontinuity handling, and a transfer step. For filtering, the next step is not more blur: a chart-aware bilateral or guided filter, irradiance-gradient reconstruction, or adaptive supersampling at high-gradient texels all preserve contact gradients better than a 3x3 binomial. Measure contact-corner bias, not only standard deviation.

### 1. Radiosity: deterministic multi-bounce GI

- Classical radiosity models object-to-object diffuse reflection and colour bleeding, treats finite-area diffuse sources, and produces surface intensities independent of the observer, so the environment can be preprocessed for dynamic sequences. — Goral, Torrance, Greenberg & Battaile, "Modeling the Interaction of Light Between Diffuse Surfaces," SIGGRAPH '84, DOI 10.1145/800031.808601, abstract.
- The classical formulation builds a form-factor matrix and solves a linear system; that is O(n²) form factors and O(n²) memory for n patches, and the Gauss-Seidel solve is also O(n²) per iteration class. — Cohen, Chen, Wallace & Greenberg, "A Progressive Refinement Approach to Fast Radiosity Image Generation," SIGGRAPH '88, DOI 10.1145/54852.378487, §2; Willmott & Heckbert, "An Empirical Comparison of Radiosity Algorithms," CMU-CS-97-115, 1997, §2 and §5.1, https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~radiosity/emprad-tr.pdf
- The hemi-cube computes object-to-object diffuse reflections in complex environments with hidden surfaces and shadows by projecting the hemisphere onto a discretized cube; it reproduces colour bleeding, shading inside shadow envelopes, and penumbras. — Cohen & Greenberg, "The Hemi-Cube: A Radiosity Solution for Complex Environments," SIGGRAPH '85, DOI 10.1145/325334.325171, abstract.
- Progressive refinement produces an initial image in time linear in the number of patches, eliminates the O(n²) memory cost by computing form factors on the fly, processes patches in sorted order by unshot energy, and adaptively subdivides elements where the gradient is too high. — Cohen et al., SIGGRAPH '88, DOI 10.1145/54852.378487, abstract, §3.2 ("Solving in Sorted Order"), §3.4 ("Adaptive Subdivision").
- Hierarchical radiosity builds a hierarchical form-factor matrix, adaptively subdivides according to a user error bound, guarantees the same precision for all form factors, and reduces the matrix to at most O(n) blocks; the authors state this removes common image artifacts caused by inaccurate form factors. — Hanrahan, Salzman & Aupperle, "A Rapid Hierarchical Radiosity Algorithm," SIGGRAPH '91, DOI 10.1145/122718.122740, abstract.
- Wavelet radiosity generalizes hierarchical radiosity into a wavelet basis, supports higher-order elements and discontinuity-aware bases, and gives linear-time hierarchical algorithms with O(n) interactions. — Gortler, Schröder, Cohen & Hanrahan, "Wavelet Radiosity," SIGGRAPH '93, DOI 10.1145/166117.166146, abstract.
- The radiosity family is deterministic: form factors come from analytic visibility or a discretized hemi-cube, and the solution is an iterative linear solve; there is no random ray sampling, so the error is discretization/aliasing error, not variance. — Goral et al. 1984, DOI 10.1145/800031.808601, abstract; Cohen & Greenberg 1985, DOI 10.1145/325334.325171, abstract; Cohen et al. 1988, DOI 10.1145/54852.378487, §2-§3.
- The characteristic radiosity artifacts are patch-resolution artifacts: blocky shadows, missing features, Mach bands, inappropriate shading discontinuities, and unresolved discontinuities. — Hanrahan, "Radiosity," Stanford CS348B Lecture 17, 2000, slides "Artifacts" and "Meshing Options," https://graphics.stanford.edu/courses/cs348b-00/lectures/lecture17/radiosity.2.pdf (lecture notes; secondary).
- Discontinuity meshing explicitly represents radiance discontinuities as mesh boundaries and uses a piecewise quadratic interpolant that preserves them; a patch mesh that does not align with a shadow boundary cannot represent the discontinuity exactly and either blurs it or resolves it only by finer subdivision. — Lischinski, Tampieri & Greenberg, "Discontinuity Meshing for Accurate Radiosity," IEEE CG&A 12(6), 1992, DOI 10.1109/38.163622, abstract.
- "Overshoot" in the radiosity literature is mainly a solver phenomenon, not a synonym for blocky patches: progressive refinement is Southwell relaxation, and overshooting (shooting more flux than the patch currently has) accelerates convergence; in the progressive scheme, the later correction can be negative unshot flux. — Gortler, Cohen & Slusallek, "Radiosity and Relaxation Methods," IEEE CG&A, 1994, DOI 10.1109/38.329094, abstract.
- The empirical comparison found that progressive radiosity with substructuring is best for simple scenes, Haar wavelet radiosity is often best for moderately complex scenes, wavelet methods use immense memory (Haar 55x the memory of substructuring; 44 MB vs 0.8 MB for the 36-chair scene; higher-order bases 150-550 MB), and higher-order wavelets produce blocky shadows with occlusion. — Willmott & Heckbert, CMU-CS-97-115, 1997, §5.1, https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~radiosity/emprad-tr.pdf
- The same comparison found that hard shadows are the weak case for wavelet/hierarchical methods: they refine less near shadow boundaries than substructuring, so hard shadows look blurrier; matrix radiosity is the most memory-intensive and is not recommended. — Willmott & Heckbert 1997, §5.1.
- Radiosity solves all diffuse bounces simultaneously through the form-factor system; progressive refinement exposes the same solution incrementally by shooting unshot energy, and each additional bounce is another transfer pass, not another stochastic sample set. — Goral et al. 1984, DOI 10.1145/800031.808601, abstract; Cohen et al. 1988, DOI 10.1145/54852.378487, abstract and §3.1.
- The cost model is therefore: O(n²) for classical matrix radiosity, O(n) memory and roughly O(n·s) for s progressive shots, O(n) links for hierarchical radiosity, and O(n) time for wavelet radiosity with O(M²) memory per element and O(M⁴) per link for order M. — Cohen et al. 1988, abstract; Hanrahan et al. 1991, abstract; Gortler et al. 1993, abstract; Willmott & Heckbert 1997, §5.1.

### 2. Shipped engines: who bakes radiosity, who path-traces, and what they store

- Quake 1's `light` tool exposes `-extra` supersampling and no radiosity bounce option; its lightmaps are direct lighting only. — id Software, `qutils/LIGHT/LIGHT.C`, lines 23, 99-102, https://github.com/id-Software/Quake-Tools/blob/master/qutils/LIGHT/LIGHT.C#L99-L102
- Quake 3's q3map2 adds opt-in radiosity: `-bounce N` re-emits surface light as diffuse lights and re-lights the map once per bounce; the default is 0 bounces. — q3map2, `light.c` lines 1807-1871 and 1969-1976, https://github.com/TTimo/GtkRadiant/blob/master/tools/quake3/q3map2/light.c#L1807-L1871; `q3map2.h` line 2183 (default `bounce = 0`), https://github.com/TTimo/GtkRadiant/blob/master/tools/quake3/q3map2/q3map2.h#L2183
- q3map2's default lightmap sample size is 16 world units, its Quake 3 lightmap page is 128x128, and its output is 8-bit RGB triples, not HDR. — q3map2, `q3map2.h` line 260 (`DEFAULT_LIGHTMAP_SAMPLE_SIZE 16`), https://github.com/TTimo/GtkRadiant/blob/master/tools/quake3/q3map2/q3map2.h#L260; `game_quake3.h` lines 113-116, https://github.com/TTimo/GtkRadiant/blob/master/tools/quake3/q3map2/game_quake3.h#L113-L116; `lightmaps_ydnar.c` line 2320 ("byte rgb triplets"), https://github.com/TTimo/GtkRadiant/blob/master/tools/quake3/q3map2/lightmaps_ydnar.c#L2320
- q3map2 also has deterministic adaptive supersampling (`-samples`), an average-light-direction "deluxe" map for normal-mapped surfaces (`-deluxe`, 3 bytes per texel), and an optional ambient-occlusion "dirt" pass (`-dirty`) that can be ordered or randomized. — q3map2, `light.c` lines 1991-1997, 2046-2048, 2282-2301, https://github.com/TTimo/GtkRadiant/blob/master/tools/quake3/q3map2/light.c#L1991-L1997; `q3map2.h` lines 277-278 (`BSP_DELUXEL_SIZE 3`), https://github.com/TTimo/GtkRadiant/blob/master/tools/quake3/q3map2/q3map2.h#L277-L278
- Valve's VRAD performs patch-based diffuse radiosity: `BounceLight` moves the direct light into the emitted term, zeroes the total, gathers light at every patch, and repeats; the default bounce cap is 100, with an early-out when the added RGB energy is below 1.0. — Valve Source SDK 2013, `src/utils/vrad/vrad.cpp`, lines 51 and 1650-1720, https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/utils/vrad/vrad.cpp
- VRAD chops surfaces into patches at a default coarsest/tightest size of 4 luxel widths (`maxchop = 4`, `minchop = 4`) and uses a 4-pass adaptive supersampling path (`extrapasses = 4`, `do_extra = true`); `-final` is equivalent to `-extrasky 16`. — Valve Source SDK 2013, `src/utils/vrad/vrad.cpp`, lines 53-55, 100-104, 2494-2518, 2820-2824, https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/utils/vrad/vrad.cpp
- VRAD's final lightmap blends the bounced light into the direct light, so Source lightmaps contain direct-plus-indirect; static brush faces do not get their direct light recomputed at runtime. — Valve Source SDK 2013, `src/utils/vrad/vrad.cpp` line 2085, https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/utils/vrad/vrad.cpp#L2085
- Source's default luxel is 16 Hammer units (`DEFAULT_LUXEL_SIZE 16.0f`); a brush face is capped at 32 luxels per axis without border and 35 including border, so VBSP subdivides oversized faces instead of allowing one giant chart. — Valve Source SDK 2013, `src/utils/common/bsplib.h` line 44, https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/utils/common/bsplib.h#L44; `src/public/bspfile.h` lines 30-35, https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/public/bspfile.h#L30-L35
- Source derives each face's lightmap axes from `lightmapVecsLuxelsPerWorldUnits`, a planar world-to-luxel projection, and stores a per-face min/size rect; faces do not share charts. — Valve Source SDK 2013, `src/utils/vrad/lightmap.cpp` lines 429-474, https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/utils/vrad/lightmap.cpp#L429-L474; `src/public/bspfile.h` (`dface_t.m_LightmapTextureMinsInLuxels` and `m_LightmapTextureSizeInLuxels`), https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/public/bspfile.h
- Source stores 4 bytes per luxel per light style, and four lightmaps per bump-lit face (base plus three bump vectors), which is the radiosity-normal-mapping layout; the HDR path uses `ColorRGBExp32` (RGBE), not float. — Valve Source SDK 2013, `src/utils/vrad/lightmap.cpp` lines 3405-3412 and 3550-3575, https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/utils/vrad/lightmap.cpp#L3405-L3412; `src/utils/vrad/lightmap.cpp` line 3027 (`SURF_BUMPLIGHT ? NUM_BUMP_VECTS + 1 : 1`), https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/utils/vrad/lightmap.cpp#L3027
- Source's radiosity-normal-mapping basis is three pairwise-orthogonal vectors whose tangent-plane projections are 120 degrees apart and whose angle to the surface normal is about 54.7 degrees, with `NUM_BUMP_VECTS 3`; the shader blends three lightmaps by the dot product of the normal-map normal with each basis vector. — Valve Source SDK 2013, `src/public/mathlib/bumpvects.h`, https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/public/mathlib/bumpvects.h; Gary McTaggart, "Half-Life 2 / Valve Source Shading," GDC 2004, slides "Basis for Radiosity Normal Mapping" and "Computing Light map Values," https://valvearchive.com/Presentations/GDC%202004/Gary%20McTaggart%20-%20Half-Life%202%20Shading/d3dtutorial10_half-life2_shading.pdf
- Valve's own course notes say radiosity normal mapping "triples light map storage" for the three directional lightmaps. — Jason Mitchell, "Shading in Valve's Source Engine," SIGGRAPH 2006 course, slide "Basis for Radiosity Normal Mapping," https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2006/Mitchell-ShadingInValvesSourceEngine.pdf
- Source's BSP compiler detects an unsealed map and writes a `.lin` leak file; `-leaktest` stops the compile when a leak is found. That is a geometry-sealing check, separate from lightmap filtering, but it is the first line of defence against light leaking in from the skybox. — Valve Source SDK 2013, `src/utils/vbsp/vbsp.cpp` lines 1239-1241 and 1325, https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/utils/vbsp/vbsp.cpp#L1239-L1241
- Unreal Lightmass emits photons based on the size of the level, uses a Lightmass Importance Volume to concentrate photons on the playable area, and gives areas outside the volume only one lower-quality bounce. — Epic Games, "CPU Lightmass Global Illumination," sections "Diffuse Interreflection" and "Lightmass Importance Volume," https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/cpu-lightmass-global-illumination-in-unreal-engine

- Unity's Beast lightmapper used Final Gather as the default integrator with an optional Path Tracer for earlier bounces; its controls included `Bounces`, `Final Gather Rays`, `Contrast Threshold` (adaptive sampling), `Interpolation` (0 linear, 1 gradient-based), `Interpolation Points`, `Resolution` in texels per world unit, and `Padding` in texels. — Unity 4.6 Manual, "Lightmapping In-Depth," section "Bake," https://docs.unity3d.com/462/Documentation/Manual/LightmappingInDepth.html

- Frostbite stores L1 spherical harmonics: 12 coefficients per texel, four RGB textures (one BC6H for L0, three BC7 or BC1 for L1), 32 bits/texel in high quality and 20 bits/texel in low quality; a 4096² lightmap is 64 MB at 32 bits or 40 MB at 20 bits. — O'Donnell, GDC 2018, section "Irradiance SH lightmap encoding," https://media.contentapi.ea.com/content/dam/eacom/frostbite/files/gdc2018-precomputedgiobalilluminationinfrostbite.pdf
- Frostbite's global chart packer handles 263k charts and ~10M used pixels into a 3316x3168 atlas at ~96% efficiency in about 10 seconds on one core; chart masks account for the 2x2 bilinear footprint, and the smallest chart is 2x2 (4x4 when bilinear), so small charts can be packed without extra padding. — O'Donnell, GDC 2018, section "Lightmap packing example" and "Ensuring correct bilinear interpolation," https://media.contentapi.ea.com/content/dam/eacom/frostbite/files/gdc2018-precomputedgiobalilluminationinfrostbite.pdf
- Frostbite discards a texel when more than 50% of its rays hit back faces (the texel is inside geometry) and fills invalid texels from the valid 8 neighbours before bilinear sampling. — O'Donnell, GDC 2018, section "Dealing with overlapping geometry" and "Ensuring correct bilinear interpolation," https://media.contentapi.ea.com/content/dam/eacom/frostbite/files/gdc2018-precomputedgiobalilluminationinfrostbite.pdf
- idTech 7's baked GI used path-traced lightmaps for static opaque geometry, 2-band spherical harmonics, and two storage variants: BC6H L0 plus 3x BC1 L1, or RGBE L0 plus 3x 32-bit L1. — Tiago Sousa, "Fast as Hell: idTech8 Global Illumination," SIGGRAPH 2025 Advances in Real-Time Rendering, slide "Past: Global Illumination on idTech 7," https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2025/content/SOUSA_SIGGRAPH_2025_Final.pdf
- idTech 7's lightmap pre-processing included unique UV unwraps, BVH generation, and lightmap LOD; the lightmap LOD is trimmed for resolution far from walkable player areas. — Sousa, SIGGRAPH 2025, slide "Past: Global Illumination on idTech 7," https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2025/content/SOUSA_SIGGRAPH_2025_Final.pdf
- idTech 7's default baked lightmap density was 2x2 texels per square metre (2 texels per linear metre, 50 cm per texel); final quality bakes took 4-10 hours per map, about 4 days for all maps, ~500 MB compressed per level and ~5 GB uncompressed; dropping to half resolution saved 4x in bake time, disk, network, and memory. — Sousa, SIGGRAPH 2025, slide "Past: Global Illumination on idTech 7," https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2025/content/SOUSA_SIGGRAPH_2025_Final.pdf
- The shipped-engine split is therefore: deterministic radiosity bakers (q3map2, VRAD), Enlighten's radiosity pipeline (ray-sampled precompute, deterministic runtime solve), and stochastic path-sampled bakers plus denoise (Lightmass, Unity Progressive, Frostbite Flux, idTech 7). harbagsa's per-texel deterministic gather is in the first family, even though it uses rays instead of a form-factor matrix. — Synthesis of the sources above; the classification follows each system's own description of its sampling (q3map2 `-bounce`, VRAD `BounceLight`, Enlighten form factors, Lightmass photons, Unity path samples, Frostbite path tracing, idTech 7 path-traced lightmaps).

### 3. Directional lightmaps, radiosity normal mapping, and SH lightmaps

- A single RGB lightmap stores one irradiance value per texel for one fixed surface normal. It is sufficient for a flat, diffuse face with no normal map; it is not sufficient when a per-pixel normal can point in different directions across the face. — Frostbite GDC 2018, section "Why spherical harmonics?" (the talk contrasts a single irradiance/radiance value with a spherical function evaluated against the runtime normal), https://media.contentapi.ea.com/content/dam/eacom/frostbite/files/gdc2018-precomputedgiobalilluminationinfrostbite.pdf
- Radiosity normal mapping stores three lightmaps in a basis of three pairwise-orthogonal vectors whose tangent-plane projections are 120 degrees apart and whose angle to the surface normal is about 54.7 degrees; at runtime the three are blended by the dot product of the normal-map normal with each basis vector. — McTaggart, GDC 2004, slides "Basis for Radiosity Normal Mapping" and "Computing Light map Values," https://valvearchive.com/Presentations/GDC%202004/Gary%20McTaggart%20-%20Half-Life%202%20Shading/d3dtutorial10_half-life2_shading.pdf; Valve Source SDK 2013, `src/public/mathlib/bumpvects.h` (`NUM_BUMP_VECTS 3`), https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/public/mathlib/bumpvects.h
- RNM triples lightmap storage for the three directional components; Source's bump-lit faces store a base lightmap plus the three bump-vector lightmaps, so the per-face lightmap cost is four times the non-bump case. — Mitchell, SIGGRAPH 2006, slide "Basis for Radiosity Normal Mapping" ("This triples light map storage"), https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2006/Mitchell-ShadingInValvesSourceEngine.pdf; Valve Source SDK 2013, `src/utils/vrad/lightmap.cpp` line 3027 and lines 3405-3412, https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/utils/vrad/lightmap.cpp#L3027
- Green's 2007 chapter extends RNM with a directional occlusion function for bump maps without extra texture memory; it is a later refinement of the same three-lightmap layout, not a different storage cost. — Green, "Efficient Self-Shadowed Radiosity Normal Mapping," SIGGRAPH 2007 Courses, DOI 10.1145/1281500.1281664, abstract.
- L1 spherical-harmonic lightmaps store 12 coefficients per texel (RGB L0 plus RGB L1), do not need a tangent frame, and can represent chroma-separated directional lighting; Frostbite encodes them in four RGB textures (BC6H L0 plus three BC7/BC1 L1), at 32 bits/texel high quality or 20 bits/texel low quality. — O'Donnell, GDC 2018, sections "Why spherical harmonics?" and "Irradiance SH lightmap encoding," https://media.contentapi.ea.com/content/dam/eacom/frostbite/files/gdc2018-precomputedgiobalilluminationinfrostbite.pdf
- A 4096² L1 SH lightmap is 64 MB at 32 bits/texel or 40 MB at 20 bits/texel; this is the memory scale that makes directional lightmaps a budget decision, not a free quality toggle. — O'Donnell, GDC 2018, section "Irradiance SH lightmap encoding," https://media.contentapi.ea.com/content/dam/eacom/frostbite/files/gdc2018-precomputedgiobalilluminationinfrostbite.pdf
- idTech 7 used 2-band SH for baked lightmaps and stored BC6H L0 plus 3x BC1 L1 (or RGBE L0 plus 3x 32-bit L1); 2-band SH was chosen as cheap and good enough for directional reconstruction. — Sousa, SIGGRAPH 2025, slide "Past: Global Illumination on idTech 7," https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2025/content/SOUSA_SIGGRAPH_2025_Final.pdf
- Unity's directional lightmap mode stores a second lightmap for the dominant incoming light direction and costs about twice the video memory of a non-directional lightmap; it is the default mode in the Lighting Settings. — Unity 6 Manual, "Lightmapping settings in the Lighting Settings Asset reference," section "Directional," https://docs.unity.com/en-us/engine/6000.0/manual/lighting-overview/direct-and-indirect-lighting/lightmapping/baking-before-runtime/lightmaps-reference
- The SH representation is compact because a Lambertian irradiance function is low-frequency: Ramamoorthi and Hanrahan show that nine coefficients (the lowest-frequency modes) approximate irradiance with average error around 1%, and Sloan et al. use 9-25 element SH vectors for diffuse transfer. — Ramamoorthi & Hanrahan, "An Efficient Representation for Irradiance Environment Maps," SIGGRAPH 2001, DOI 10.1145/383259.383317, abstract; Sloan, Kautz & Snyder, "Precomputed Radiance Transfer for Real-Time Rendering in Dynamic, Low-Frequency Lighting Environments," SIGGRAPH 2002, DOI 10.1145/566570.566612, abstract.
- Directional storage is unnecessary for harbagsa's static boxes: every box face is planar and has one normal for the whole chart, and the project has no surface or material textures, so one RGB irradiance value per texel is the correct amount of data. — `docs/adr/0045-mixed-static-baked-indirect.md` ("The flat box look"; "A surface or material texture. Rejected... no surface or material textures").
- Dynamic boxes already get a directional representation from the probe field (8x8 octahedral irradiance plus depth moments); the static RGB lightmap and the dynamic directional probe are a coherent split, not a missing feature. — `src/bake/bake.odin` lines 24-30 (`PROBE_TEXELS 8`, `PROBE_IRRADIANCE_CHANNELS 4`, `PROBE_MOMENT_CHANNELS 2`); `docs/adr/0044-bake-data-contract.md` ("Probes ... 8 x 8 octahedral irradiance block and an 8 x 8 first/second moment block").
- The cost of adding directional/SH lightmaps to harbagsa would be 2x-4x the lightmap memory and bandwidth (Unity's second map is 2x; RNM is 3x; Source's base-plus-three is 4x; L1 SH is 4 RGB textures), plus bake work, for a scene with no normal maps. That would be over-engineering, not a quality win. — Synthesis of the costs above; the project's flat-face/no-material-texture constraint is from `docs/adr/0045-mixed-static-baked-indirect.md`.

### 4. Deterministic filtering and denoising for baked lightmaps

- Irradiance gradients predict how irradiance changes with position and surface orientation from the same hemispherical samples; Ward and Heckbert report that gradient-based interpolation produces smoother, more accurate renderings by using the samples better rather than by taking more samples or changing the sampling strategy. — Ward & Heckbert, "Irradiance Gradients," Third Eurographics Workshop on Rendering, 1992, pp. 85-98, abstract, https://radsite.lbl.gov/radiance/papers/erw92/paper.html
- Irradiance caching/interpolation is the context: the 1988 ray-tracing paper introduced the sparse diffuse-interreflection cache, and the 1992 paper introduced the gradients used to interpolate it. — Ward, Rubinstein & Clear, "A Ray Tracing Solution for Diffuse Interreflection," SIGGRAPH '88, DOI 10.1145/378456.378490, abstract; Ward & Heckbert 1992, abstract.
- Beast exposed gradient-based interpolation as a user setting (`Interpolation`: 0 linear, 1 advanced gradient-based) and used `Contrast Threshold` to decide where new final-gather points are needed; it is a shipped example of gradient interpolation rather than a fixed blur. — Unity 4.6 Manual, "Lightmapping In-Depth," section "Bake," https://docs.unity3d.com/462/Documentation/Manual/LightmappingInDepth.html
- Source VRAD's deterministic approach to aliased shadows is adaptive supersampling rather than a post-blur: `do_extra` is true by default, `extrapasses` is 4, samples are supersampled where the lighting is non-uniform, and `-final` is equivalent to `-extrasky 16`; `-noextra` disables it. — Valve Source SDK 2013, `src/utils/vrad/vrad.cpp`, lines 100-104, 2494-2518, 2820-2824, https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/utils/vrad/vrad.cpp; `src/utils/vrad/lightmap.cpp`, lines 2868-2967, https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/utils/vrad/lightmap.cpp
- Unity's Progressive Lightmapper offers a Gaussian filter described as "a bilateral Gaussian filter" and an A-Trous filter that is edge-aware; the docs say the Gaussian blurs the lightmap and reduces visible noise, while A-Trous minimizes blur while removing noise. — Unity 6 Manual, "Lightmapping settings in the Lighting Settings Asset reference," sections "Filtering" and "Advanced Filtering settings," https://docs.unity.com/en-us/engine/6000.0/manual/lighting-overview/direct-and-indirect-lighting/lightmapping/baking-before-runtime/lightmaps-reference
- harbagsa's filter is a separable 3x3 binomial with one-dimensional weights `[1 2 1]/4`, applied per chart after the buried fill and before the gutter; the weights sum to one, and the chart is one planar face, so the kernel cannot cross a surface. — `src/bake/denoise.odin` lines 4-8 and 48-50; `docs/adr/0045-mixed-static-baked-indirect.md` ("Denoise").
- A symmetric 3x3 low-pass necessarily averages across a contact gradient: where irradiance changes rapidly over roughly one texel, the filter lifts the darker side and darkens the brighter side. At 4 texels/m the kernel radius is 25 cm. — Mathematical consequence of the normalized `[1 2 1]/4` kernel; the measured result is in `docs/evidence/mixed-lighting.md` ("the one-texel filter softens contact corners by one texel").
- The measured effect is real but modest: the 3x3 binomial cuts the clean-window standard deviation from 0.0006996 to 0.0002706 (-61% linear) and the linear gradient from 0.00003166 to 0.00000762 (-76%), while the chart-interior mean stays within 0.2% and the linear band mean moves 1.1%; the contact corners soften by one texel. — `docs/evidence/mixed-lighting.md`, section "Denoise," tables "scene_color, linear" and "Accepted limitations."
- A 5x5 Gaussian was measured on the same seed and rejected: it improved the clean-window standard deviation to 0.795 (-60%) but softened contact corners by two texels (50 cm) instead of one, and the whole-band number stayed far from half. More blur was not the limiting metric. — `docs/evidence/mixed-lighting.md`, section "Denoise" ("A 5x5 Gaussian was measured on the same seed as the escalation and rejected").
- A bilateral filter weights neighbours by both spatial distance and photometric similarity, so it smooths within a region while preserving edges; it is the natural deterministic replacement for a plain binomial when the signal has contact gradients. — Tomasi & Manduchi, "Bilateral Filtering for Gray and Color Images," ICCV 1998, DOI 10.1109/ICCV.1998.710815, abstract.
- A guided filter uses a guidance image in a local linear model, behaves like an edge-preserving smoother with better behaviour near edges than the bilateral filter, and has a linear-time algorithm whose cost is independent of kernel size; for a lightmap the guide can be the chart's position or the irradiance itself. — He, Sun & Tang, "Guided Image Filtering," ECCV 2010, DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-15549-9_1, abstract.
- The edge-avoiding A-Trous wavelet filter is a multi-scale edge-aware filter designed for ray-traced Monte Carlo GI; it is usually paired with a stochastic renderer, but the filter itself is deterministic and can be applied to a baked lightmap if the edge weights use the lightmap's own gradients. — Dammertz, Sewtz, Hanika & Lensch, "Edge-Avoiding A-Trous Wavelet Transform for Fast Global Illumination Filtering," HPG 2010, pp. 67-75, https://www.highperformancegraphics.org/previous/www_2010/media/RayTracing_I/HPG2010_RayTracing_I_Dammertz.pdf
- The alternative to filtering is more deterministic sampling at the places that need it: Source supersamples non-uniform samples; a lightmap baker can spend 4x rays on texels near a contact gradient and leave smooth texels at 128 rays. That preserves the contact instead of averaging it away, at a cost proportional to the fraction of high-gradient texels. — Valve Source SDK 2013, `src/utils/vrad/lightmap.cpp` lines 2868-2967 (supersampling only where needed), https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/utils/vrad/lightmap.cpp#L2868-L2967
- For harbagsa, the cheapest quality upgrade is therefore a chart-aware bilateral or guided filter, or gradient-based reconstruction, not a larger Gaussian; the current 3x3 is a good baseline but its one-texel contact bias is the measurable cost. — Synthesis of Ward & Heckbert 1992, Tomasi & Manduchi 1998, He et al. 2010, and the harbagsa evidence above.

### 5. Quality and performance budgets, and production failure modes

- Source's default is 16 Hammer units per luxel; with the common 1 unit = 1 inch convention that is 40.6 cm per texel (2.46 texels/m), and with the 1 unit = 0.75 inch convention it is 30.5 cm per texel (3.28 texels/m). The engine number is exact; the metre conversion depends on the unit convention. — Valve Source SDK 2013, `src/utils/common/bsplib.h` line 44 (`DEFAULT_LUXEL_SIZE 16.0f`), https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/utils/common/bsplib.h#L44; the unit convention conflict is documented in the **Conflicts** section below.
- q3map2's default sample size is also 16 world units, and its Quake 3 lightmap page is 128x128; at the common Quake convention of 1 unit = 1 inch that is about 40.6 cm per sample (2.46 texels/m). — q3map2, `q3map2.h` line 260 and `game_quake3.h` lines 113-116, https://github.com/TTimo/GtkRadiant/blob/master/tools/quake3/q3map2/q3map2.h#L260 and https://github.com/TTimo/GtkRadiant/blob/master/tools/quake3/q3map2/game_quake3.h#L113-L116
- Unity's default lightmap resolution is 40 texels per world unit (2.5 cm per texel), with a 2-texel padding and a default 1024 maximum lightmap size; that is much finer than Source, q3map2, Frostbite, or idTech 7, and it is an editor default rather than a shipped-game density. — Unity 6 Scripting API, `LightingSettings.lightmapResolution` (default 40), https://docs.unity.com/en-us/engine/6000.0/script-reference/unityengine/lightingsettings/lightmapresolution; Unity 6 Manual, "Lightmapping settings in the Lighting Settings Asset reference," sections "Lightmap Padding" and "Max Lightmap Size," https://docs.unity.com/en-us/engine/6000.0/manual/lighting-overview/direct-and-indirect-lighting/lightmapping/baking-before-runtime/lightmaps-reference
- Frostbite's *Battlefront II* target was about 20-25 cm per texel (4-5 texels/m), and idTech 7's default was 2x2 texels per square metre (2 texels per linear metre, 50 cm per texel); the production range is therefore roughly 2-5 texels/m for large shipped games, with Unity's editor default an outlier at 40 texels/m. — O'Donnell, GDC 2018, slide "Lightmap density," https://media.contentapi.ea.com/content/dam/eacom/frostbite/files/gdc2018-precomputedgiobalilluminationinfrostbite.pdf; Sousa, SIGGRAPH 2025, slide "Past: Global Illumination on idTech 7," https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2025/content/SOUSA_SIGGRAPH_2025_Final.pdf
- Source's per-face cap is 32 luxels per axis (35 with border); VBSP subdivides a face that would exceed it. The cap bounds the largest chart and forces a finer mesh of charts on big surfaces, at the cost of more chart boundaries. — Valve Source SDK 2013, `src/public/bspfile.h` lines 30-35, https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/public/bspfile.h#L30-L35
- Frostbite's global lightmap atlas example packs 263k charts and ~10M used pixels into 3316x3168 at ~96% efficiency; the packer uses chart masks that already include the 2x2 bilinear footprint, so it does not need a separate 4-texel gutter around every chart. — O'Donnell, GDC 2018, sections "Lightmap packing example" and "Ensuring correct bilinear interpolation," https://media.contentapi.ea.com/content/dam/eacom/frostbite/files/gdc2018-precomputedgiobalilluminationinfrostbite.pdf
- Unity's lightmap padding default is 2 texels, and its docs require at least two full texels between UV charts to avoid light bleeding because a bilinear sample reads the four nearest texels; the bilinear neighbourhood, not the chart rectangle, is the thing that must not overlap. — Unity Manual, "Fix light bleeding in lightmaps," section "Cause," https://docs.unity3d.com/6000.0/Documentation/Manual/ProgressiveLightmapper-UVOverlap.html; Unity 6 Manual, "Troubleshooting automatically generated lightmap UVs," https://docs.unity.com/en-us/engine/6000.0/manual/lighting-overview/direct-and-indirect-lighting/lightmapping/lighting-gi-uvs/troubleshooting
- Source's bounce count is a cap of 100 with an early-out at added RGB < 1.0; q3map2 defaults to 0 bounces and only bounces when `-bounce` is passed; Unity's Progressive Lightmapper defaults to 2 bounces (max 100); Unreal exposes a configurable bounce count and says bounce 1 dominates; Frostbite and idTech 7 path-trace multiple bounces. One bounce is at the low end of shipped practice, not outside it. — Valve `vrad.cpp` lines 51 and 1717; q3map2 `q3map2.h` line 2183 and `light.c` lines 1969-1976; Unity 6 "Bounces" section; Epic "Make Lightmass World Info Settings" (`Num Indirect Lighting Bounces`); O'Donnell GDC 2018 and Sousa SIGGRAPH 2025.
- idTech 7's final quality bakes took 4-10 hours per map, about 4 days for all maps, ~500 MB compressed per level, ~5 GB uncompressed, and ~1.4 TB of network storage; dropping to half resolution saved 4x. These are the storage and iteration costs that frame idTech 8's move away from baked GI. — Sousa, SIGGRAPH 2025, slide "Past: Global Illumination on idTech 7," https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2025/content/SOUSA_SIGGRAPH_2025_Final.pdf
- Frostbite's example cost is 8 megapixels at 100k rays/texel, roughly 800 billion rays and more than 200 CPU core-hours; the talk explicitly notes that some scenes need bidirectional path tracing, Metropolis light transport, photon mapping, or radiosity to converge. — O'Donnell, GDC 2018, slide "Path tracing cost," https://media.contentapi.ea.com/content/dam/eacom/frostbite/files/gdc2018-precomputedgiobalilluminationinfrostbite.pdf
- Epic describes Lightmass build time as "a few minutes or possibly substantially longer" depending on lights, quality, level size, importance volume, and Swarm machines; the exact wall time is content-dependent, not a fixed benchmark. — Epic Games, "Lightmass Basics," section "Building," https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/lightmass-basics-in-unreal-engine
- The most common lightmap failure modes in these sources are: insufficient chart margin (bilinear neighbourhood overlap), texels inside geometry (back-face rays), light leaking through unsealed or thin geometry, over-smoothing that erases indirect shadows, blocky/patchy radiosity meshes, and running out of atlas/page memory. — Unity light-bleed docs; Frostbite "Dealing with overlapping geometry"; Epic "Lightmass Importance Volume" and "Indirect Lighting Smoothness"; Lischinski et al. 1992; Valve `bspfile.h` 32-luxel cap; Unity "Max Lightmap Size."
- Over-smoothing is an explicit production trade: Lightmass's `Indirect Lighting Smoothness` hides solver noise but loses detailed indirect shadows; Unity's Gaussian filter blurs more than A-Trous while A-Trous is edge-aware; Beast's `Interpolation Points` can smooth out details. The lesson is that a filter is a quality decision, not a free denoise. — Epic "CPU Lightmass Global Illumination," section "World Settings" (`Indirect Lighting Smoothness`); Unity 6 "Filtering"; Unity 4.6 "Lightmapping In-Depth," section "Bake" (`Interpolation Points`).
- Source's per-face 32-luxel cap, Unity's max lightmap size, Unreal's packed light/shadow map size, and Frostbite's global atlas are all constraints on how much lightmap data one chart or one atlas can hold; when the budget fills, the engine subdivides, pages, compresses, or fails. — Valve `bspfile.h`; Unity "Max Lightmap Size"; Epic "World Settings" (`Packed Light and Shadow Map Texture Size`); O'Donnell GDC 2018, section "Lightmap packing example."
- The production lesson for harbagsa is that 4 texels/m is not the weak point. Frostbite targeted 20-25 cm/texel and idTech 7 defaulted to 50 cm/texel; harbagsa is at 25 cm. The weak points are the one-bounce truncation, the fixed low-pass filter, and the atlas padding. — Synthesis of the density sources above and the harbagsa constants below.

### 6. How harbagsa's static bake compares

- harbagsa's lightmap is static indirect only: direct light and hard ray-query shadows stay real-time, the bake traces one diffuse bounce, a miss returns zero, and there is no sky/sun/ambient term. Dynamic lamps and dynamic crates do not enter the bake; static fixtures and static emissive boxes do. — `README.md` ("a baked one-bounce static indirect"); `docs/adr/0045-mixed-static-baked-indirect.md`, sections "Decisions" and "One bounce."
- The bake defaults are 4 texels/m, a 1024² atlas, 8 bytes/texel (RGBA16F), a 4-texel gutter, an 8-texel minimum chart, 128 lightmap rays/texel, 1 bounce, 1 m probes (40x5x40), 1024 probe rays, and 8x8 octahedral irradiance plus depth moments. — `src/bake/bake.odin` lines 17-33; `docs/adr/0044-bake-data-contract.md` ("Atlas" and "Probes").
- The measured default bake is 5.06 s total at 16 threads: 3.0-3.14 s for the lightmap stage, 1.8-2.0 s for the probe stage, 31-33 ms for encode, 14,608,480 bytes of artifact, 37.7 MB peak RSS, and 165,334 valid texels. — `docs/evidence/mixed-lighting.md`, section "Bake," table "Measure."
- The runtime indirect term costs about 0.03 ms at 1080p; the full frame at 96 lights is 1.50-1.52 ms GPU total p50 and 1.83-1.85 ms frame interval p50 against an 8.0 ms budget. Quality can therefore be increased at bake time without threatening the frame budget. — `docs/evidence/mixed-lighting.md`, section "Performance," table "Variant"; `docs/evidence/perf-baseline.md`.
- The bake is deterministic: the key hashes canonical static boxes, active static fixtures, quality, and tool version; the seed is fixed; three 1 m runs produced identical bytes and the same key/payload/md5; the texel seed follows `(source, id, face, x, y)`, so moving a chart does not re-roll its noise. — `docs/evidence/mixed-lighting.md`, section "Bake" ("The three 1 m runs write identical bytes") and section "Denoise" ("The seed change is measurable on its own"); `docs/adr/0044-bake-data-contract.md` ("Key").
- The quality evidence is strong on seams and leaks: a sealed four-wall 4x4 m cell showed the outer face mean at 16.71099091 in both bakes (identical at the printed precision) across a fixture removal, with the crate face moving by at most 0.08 code values; the stair/floor-wall seam comparison showed no outlier line. Those are specific cases, not a proof for every coplanar wall segment or every thin wall. — `docs/evidence/mixed-lighting.md`, quality table ("No wall leak," "No lightmap seam").
- The 3x3 binomial is the measured contact-bias cost: it cuts the clean-window standard deviation from 0.0006996 to 0.0002706 linear (-61%) and cuts the linear gradient by 76%, while keeping the chart-interior mean within 0.2%, moving the linear band mean by 1.1%, and softening contact corners by one texel (25 cm). — `docs/evidence/mixed-lighting.md`, section "Denoise," tables "scene_color, linear" and "Accepted limitations."
- The 5x5 escalation was measured and rejected because it softened contact corners by two texels (50 cm) instead of one, even though it reduced the clean-window standard deviation slightly more; this is direct evidence that the current filter is already at the point of diminishing returns for a fixed low-pass. — `docs/evidence/mixed-lighting.md`, section "Denoise" ("A 5x5 Gaussian was measured on the same seed as the escalation and rejected").
- harbagsa's 4 texels/m (25 cm/texel) is at the Frostbite *Battlefront II* target (20-25 cm), about 1.2-1.6x finer than Source's default (30-41 cm), and 2x finer than idTech 7's default (50 cm). It is 10x coarser than Unity's 40 texels/unit editor default, but that default is for a different scale of scene. The density is not the quality problem. — Frostbite GDC 2018, "Lightmap density"; Valve `bsplib.h` 44; Sousa SIGGRAPH 2025, "Past: Global Illumination on idTech 7"; Unity `lightmapResolution` API.
- The 15.8% valid-texel fraction is not packing efficiency. At 4 texels/m, a 1 m face has 4x4 = 16 useful texels but is forced to an 8x8 = 64-texel minimum chart inside a 16x16 = 256-texel allocation (25% chart efficiency); a 4 m face is 16x16 = 256 useful inside 24x24 = 576 (44%); an 8 m face is 32x32 = 1024 inside 40x40 = 1600 (64%). The current 165,334 valid texels therefore occupy roughly 0.4-0.7M of the 1.05M atlas texels, i.e. the atlas is about 35-63% allocated. — Derived from `src/bake/bake.odin` (`TEXELS_PER_METER 4`, `GUTTER_TEXELS 4`, `MIN_CHART_TEXELS 8`) and `docs/evidence/mixed-lighting.md` (`valid_texels 165,334`); this is an estimate, not a measured occupancy.
- With the current padding, a 2x linear arena (4x surface area) would need about 1.6-2.6M atlas texels and would not fit the 1024² atlas; with 96% packing the same content would need about 689k texels and would fit. The cheapest growth path is therefore to fix the padding/packing before enlarging the atlas. — Derived from the same constants and the Frostbite 96% packing figure (O'Donnell GDC 2018, "Lightmap packing example"); first-order estimate.
- The 32 MiB artifact budget makes a single 2048² RGBA16F atlas impossible in practice: 2048² x 8 bytes = 32 MiB (33.55 MB) for the lightmap alone, exactly the budget before the probe atlas. Two 1024² pages (16 MiB of lightmap, about 21.9 MiB total with the 1 m probes at 5.86 MiB) fit; a 4-byte HDR encoding such as Source's RGBE would make a 2048² atlas 16 MiB and also fit. BC6H at 8 bpp would be 4 MiB but requires format support. — Arithmetic from `src/bake/bake.odin` (`ATLAS_BYTES_PER_TEXEL 8`, `ATLAS_SIZE 1024`) and `docs/evidence/mixed-lighting.md` (`gpu_bytes 14,608,096`, 32 MiB budget); Source RGBE from `vrad` `ColorRGBExp32`; Unity BC6H 8 bpp from Unity "Lightmap data format."
- One chart per face is a good seam decision for a box arena: each chart is planar, the filter cannot cross a surface, and the 4-texel gutter isolates neighbouring charts. It is not a general UV solution: coplanar wall segments made of separate boxes get separate charts, and a shared edge can in principle show a value discontinuity; the current seam evidence covers stairs and a floor-wall junction, not a long coplanar wall seam. — `src/bake/atlas.odin` (one chart per face, shelf packing); `docs/evidence/mixed-lighting.md` ("No lightmap seam"); the coplanar-wall case is **Not found**.
- The probe field is expensive relative to the lightmap: 8,000 probes x 1024 rays = 8.19M rays for 1.8-2.0 s, versus 165,334 texels x 128 rays = 21.16M rays for 3.0-3.14 s. The probe stage is about 38% of the bake time for a term only dynamic boxes sample; the 2 m fallback reduces it but still lands at about 3.4 s total. — `docs/evidence/mixed-lighting.md`, section "Bake" ("Probe stage 1854-2012 ms," "2 m probe fallback ... 3.4 s"); `src/bake/bake.odin` (`PROBE_COUNTS`).
- The probe field is interpolated, not solved, at runtime: dynamic boxes sample the four probes of the containing tetrahedron with normal bias, a facing weight, and a baked depth-moment test, while static boxes never read the probes. The runtime cost is interpolation, so there is no per-frame GI noise to denoise. — `docs/adr/0044-bake-data-contract.md` ("Probes" and "Runtime resources").
- The 2 s bake target is missed by about 2.5x; the lightmap stage alone is about 3.0 s, so no probe-only optimization can reach 2 s. Thread scaling is 37.5 s at 1 thread, 9.8 s at 4, 6.3 s at 8, 5.8 s at 12, and 5.0 s at 16. The 5 s default is fast by production standards; the target is aggressive, not the bake. — `docs/evidence/mixed-lighting.md`, section "Bake," thread-scaling paragraph and failure note.
- Over-engineered for this arena: the 4-texel gutter (a 1-texel border is enough for the bilinear footprint), the 8-texel minimum chart (4 is enough for a 3x3 chart-local filter), and RGBA16F at 1024² (fine now, but it blocks a single 2048² atlas once the probes are included). The probe stage is separately expensive for its role: 1024 rays per probe for a term only dynamic boxes sample. — Derived from the constants and the cited production padding/packing figures.
- Under-engineered for the stated look: one bounce (no second-order colour bleeding), a fixed low-pass filter (one-texel contact bias), a single atlas page (no growth), no maximum chart size/subdivision (a very large face can dominate the atlas), and no mip chain (minification filtering is left to TAA). — Derived from ADR 0045, `src/bake/atlas.odin`, and the production sources above; the mip impact is **Not found**.
- A second per-texel bounce would add about 3.0 s to the lightmap stage (another 21.16M rays), taking the total to about 8.1 s; a third would take it to about 11.1 s. A patch-level solve can add bounces more cheaply, but that is an implementation change, not a quality flag. — Arithmetic from the measured lightmap stage and ray count; first-order estimate.
- A chart-aware bilateral/guided filter or gradient reconstruction would cost far less than a bounce (a 5x5 filter is 25 taps per texel, negligible against 128 rays) and would target the measured contact bias rather than the global standard deviation. — Synthesis of Ward & Heckbert 1992, Tomasi & Manduchi 1998, He et al. 2010, and the harbagsa denoise evidence.

### 7. What a noise-free multi-bounce or better-filtered bake would look like

- The constraint excludes real-time path-traced GI and stochastic sampling and temporal denoise; it does not exclude deterministic offline ray sampling, which the current bake already uses. A noise-free multi-bounce bake therefore has to be a deterministic radiosity/gather: fixed sample sets, fixed seed, no per-frame or per-bake random sequence, and no temporal accumulation. — Project constraint; the classical radiosity sources above (Goral 1984, Cohen 1988, Hanrahan 1991, Gortler 1993) are the noise-free reference.
- Design A, the smallest change: keep the current 128-ray first-bounce gather, store the first-bounce lightmap, then do a second deterministic pass that traces 128 rays per texel and samples the first-bounce lightmap at each hit, multiplied by the hit surface's albedo; repeat for bounce 3. This is a deterministic lightmap-feedback radiosity gather using the same offline ray sampling the current bake already uses, not a stochastic path tracer, and it reuses the existing BVH and chart layout. — Design synthesis from Goral et al. 1984 (interreflection as a transfer between surface patches) and the harbagsa bake structure (`src/bake/lightmap.odin`, `src/bake/bvh.odin`); the implementation detail is a proposal, not a cited result.
- Design A cost: the first bounce is 165,334 x 128 = 21.16M rays and 3.0-3.14 s; each additional per-texel bounce adds about the same, so two bounces are about 6.0 s of lightmap stage and three are about 9.0 s, before the probe and encode stages. This is the simple option, not the cheap one. — Arithmetic from `docs/evidence/mixed-lighting.md` ("165,334 texels" and "Lightmap stage 3016-3140 ms").
- Design B, the classical option: build a patch mesh over each face (one patch per face, or a quadtree), compute form factors with a deterministic hemi-cube or fixed ray set, solve progressive radiosity by shooting from the patch with the greatest unshot energy, and interpolate the converged patch radiosities to the lightmap texels. This is the Goral/Cohen/Hanrahan family and gives all bounces from one solve. — Goral et al. 1984, DOI 10.1145/800031.808601; Cohen et al. 1988, DOI 10.1145/54852.378487, §3.2 and §3.4; Hanrahan et al. 1991, DOI 10.1145/122718.122740, abstract.
- Design B cost: classical matrix radiosity is O(n²); progressive refinement is O(n) memory with one hemi-cube per shot; hierarchical radiosity reduces the matrix to O(n) links with an error bound. For a few thousand box faces, a hierarchical/progressive patch solve is likely cheaper than a per-texel second bounce, but it needs a patch hierarchy, visibility, and a transfer step. — Cohen et al. 1988, abstract; Hanrahan et al. 1991, abstract; Willmott & Heckbert 1997, §5.1.
- Design B artifacts: patch resolution produces blocky shadows, missing features, Mach bands, and unresolved discontinuities; discontinuity meshing explicitly represents the radiance discontinuities, and adaptive subdivision refines where the gradient is high. For a box arena, the hard edges are already chart boundaries, but contact shadows still need sub-face resolution. — Hanrahan CS348B Lecture 17, "Artifacts" and "Meshing Options"; Lischinski et al. 1992, DOI 10.1109/38.163622, abstract; Cohen et al. 1988, §3.4.
- Design C, the filter: replace the 3x3 binomial with a chart-aware bilateral filter (spatial sigma about one texel, range sigma tied to local contrast), a guided filter using the chart's position/irradiance as guidance, or irradiance-gradient reconstruction. All are deterministic given the baked input; the bilateral/guided filter preserves the contact edge, and the gradient reconstruction predicts the change instead of averaging it. — Tomasi & Manduchi 1998, DOI 10.1109/ICCV.1998.710815, abstract; He et al. 2010, DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-15549-9_1, abstract; Ward & Heckbert 1992, abstract.
- Design C alternative: adaptive deterministic supersampling at high-gradient texels. Source does this with `-extra`/`-final`; a lightmap baker can spend 4x rays only near contact gradients and leave smooth texels at 128. This preserves the contact instead of blurring it, at a cost proportional to the high-gradient area. — Valve Source SDK 2013, `src/utils/vrad/vrad.cpp` lines 100-104 and 2820-2824, and `lightmap.cpp` lines 2868-2967.
- Design D, the atlas: reduce the gutter from 4 to 1 texel (the bilinear footprint needs one), reduce the minimum chart from 8 to 4 (the 3x3 filter needs a 3x3 neighbourhood), and pack with a perimeter-sorted shelf or a chart-mask/bitmask packer. Frostbite's chart-mask packer reports ~96% on 263k charts; the current shelf packer plus 4-texel gutter is the main reason the atlas is only ~35-63% allocated. — O'Donnell GDC 2018, "Ensuring correct bilinear interpolation" and "Lightmap packing example"; `src/bake/atlas.odin`; derived occupancy estimate above.
- Design D alternative for growth: add a second 1024² RGBA16F page (16 MiB of lightmap, about 21.9 MiB total with the 1 m probes at 5.86 MiB) before raising the single atlas to 2048² (32 MiB, exactly the budget before probes, so over in practice); or switch the lightmap to a 4-byte HDR encoding such as RGBE (2048² = 16 MiB) or BC6H at 8 bpp (2048² = 4 MiB) if the Vulkan format path is available. — Arithmetic from `src/bake/bake.odin` (`ATLAS_BYTES_PER_TEXEL 8`, `ATLAS_SIZE 1024`) and `docs/evidence/mixed-lighting.md` (14,608,096 bytes, 32 MiB budget); Source RGBE from `vrad` `ColorRGBExp32`; Unity BC6H 8 bpp from Unity "Lightmap data format."
- Design E, directional storage: do not add it. The static boxes are flat, one normal per face, no normal maps; a single RGB irradiance lightmap is the correct representation. RNM/SH would add 2x-4x memory and bake cost for no visible benefit on flat faces. — `docs/adr/0045-mixed-static-baked-indirect.md` ("The flat box look"; "A surface or material texture. Rejected"); the directional costs above (Mitchell 2006, O'Donnell 2018, Unity directional mode).
- Success metrics for a better bake: contact-corner bias (the code value at a floor-wall or crate-floor junction), seam/leak deltas on sealed and coplanar cases, clean-window standard deviation, chart-interior mean energy, bake wall time, atlas occupancy, and the number of charts that hit the min-chart floor. Reporting only standard deviation hides the contact bias that the filter introduces. — Synthesis of the harbagsa evidence metrics (`docs/evidence/mixed-lighting.md`) and the production filtering sources (Unity "Filtering," Lightmass "Indirect Lighting Smoothness," Beast "Interpolation Points").
- Expected costs: a 5x5 bilateral or guided filter is 25 taps per texel, negligible against 128 rays; adaptive supersampling at 5-20% of texels adds about 0.1-0.6 s; a per-texel second bounce adds about 3.0 s; a patch-level solve is implementation-heavy but can be under a second for a few thousand faces; a 96% packer is a build-time cost (Frostbite reports ~10 s for 263k charts on one core). These are order-of-magnitude estimates, not measurements on harbagsa. — Arithmetic from the harbagsa ray counts; Frostbite packing time from O'Donnell GDC 2018, "Lightmap packing example."
- What not to do: path tracing or a temporal denoiser (excluded by the constraint); a 5x5 Gaussian (measured to soften two texels); a single 2048² RGBA16F atlas (32 MiB before probes, over with them); SH/RNM on flat faces (unnecessary); a second bounce at full per-texel cost if a patch solve can do it cheaper. — harbagsa evidence ("5x5 Gaussian ... rejected"); budget arithmetic; flat-face constraint; radiosity cost sources.

### 8. Hard limits of the fixed atlas as the arena grows

- Raw capacity: 1024² = 1,048,576 texels; at 4 texels/m that is 16 texels/m², so 100% packing would cover 65,536 m² of valid surface. The current 165,334 valid texels cover 10,333 m², which is 15.8% of the atlas and 6.3x raw headroom. — Arithmetic from `src/bake/bake.odin` (`ATLAS_SIZE 1024`, `TEXELS_PER_METER 4`) and `docs/evidence/mixed-lighting.md` (`valid_texels 165,334`).
- The 15.8% is not packing efficiency. The 4-texel gutter and 8-texel min chart turn a 1 m face into a 16x16 allocation for 8x8 valid texels (25% chart efficiency), a 4 m face into 24x24 for 16x16 (44%), and an 8 m face into 40x40 for 32x32 (64%). At the arena's mix of small and medium boxes, the current content plausibly occupies 0.4-0.7M of the 1.05M atlas texels, i.e. 35-63% allocated. — Derived from `src/bake/atlas.odin` (chart width = max(min_chart, ceil(metres x 4)), allocation = width + 2 x gutter) and the valid-texel count; this is an estimate, not a measured `dilated_texels`/occupancy number.
- Growth scenario 1: a 2x linear arena has 4x the surface area, so about 661k valid texels. At 96% packing that is about 689k atlas texels and fits the 1024² atlas; at the current 25-44% chart efficiency it is 1.5-2.6M and does not fit. Better packing alone can therefore carry a 2x linear growth. — Arithmetic from the valid-texel count and the Frostbite 96% packing figure; first-order estimate that assumes the same density, padding, and surface-area distribution.
- Growth scenario 2: a 2.5x linear arena has 6.25x the surface area, about 1.03M valid texels; at 96% packing that is about 1.07M atlas texels, just over the 1024² capacity. A slightly larger atlas or a second page is needed at that point. — Same arithmetic; first-order estimate.
- Growth scenario 3: a 4x linear arena has 16x the surface area, about 2.65M valid texels; at 96% packing that is about 2.76M texels, which needs roughly a 1664² atlas (about 21.1 MiB RGBA16F) plus the 1 m probes (5.86 MiB), total about 27.0 MiB, still under 32 MiB. A 5x linear arena is about 4.13M valid texels, about 4.3M atlas texels, which needs 2048² (32 MiB) and is over budget with the probes. — Arithmetic; first-order estimate.
- The 32 MiB artifact budget is therefore a second hard limit: a single 2048² RGBA16F lightmap is 32 MiB, exactly the budget before the probes, so it does not fit with them; the largest square RGBA16F atlas that fits with the 1 m probe data is roughly 1800² (about 24.7 MiB for the lightmap plus 5.86 MiB of probes, about 30.6 MiB). Two 1024² pages fit; a 4-byte HDR format at 2048² fits; BC6H at 8 bpp fits if supported. — Arithmetic from `ATLAS_BYTES_PER_TEXEL 8`, `ATLAS_SIZE 1024`, and the measured 14,608,096-byte artifact; Source RGBE and Unity BC6H sizes as above.
- The third hard limit is bake time: the lightmap stage is about 6.7-7.0M rays/s on this machine (21.16M rays in 3.0-3.14 s); doubling the texel count or adding a bounce doubles the stage, and the 16-thread scaling is 5.0 s total versus 37.5 s at one thread. A larger arena at the same density and ray count is roughly linear in valid texels, not free. — `docs/evidence/mixed-lighting.md`, section "Bake," thread-scaling and lightmap-stage numbers.
- The fixed atlas is not the first limit; the padding is. For the current arena, the 1024² atlas is sufficient and the 15.8% valid fraction is mostly padding/min-chart waste, not a shortage of texels. For a 2x linear arena, better packing is enough; for 2.5x, add a page; for 4x, a resizable atlas plus RGBA16F is just under budget; for 5x, change the format or lower the density. — Synthesis of the arithmetic above.
- Lowering the density is the other lever: at 2 texels/m (50 cm/texel, idTech 7's default) the same atlas covers 4x the surface area, but the 3x3 filter would then soften contacts by 50 cm, and the flat box look would show it. At 4 texels/m, the 25 cm texel is already at the Frostbite target; lowering density is a last resort, not the first. — Sousa SIGGRAPH 2025, "Past: Global Illumination on idTech 7"; Frostbite GDC 2018, "Lightmap density"; harbagsa 3x3 filter evidence.
- The surface-area scaling is first-order: a larger arena may add more small boxes, more coplanar wall segments, and more charts, which makes the min-chart/gutter overhead worse; it may also add large open floors, which are more efficient. The numbers above are a planning estimate, not a guarantee. — Derived; stated as an assumption.
- The practical order of work is: (1) reduce gutter/min chart and pack better; (2) add a second page or a 4-byte HDR format; (3) add a deterministic second bounce or a patch solve; (4) only then raise the atlas size or lower the density. This order gets the most quality per byte and per second. — Synthesis of the packing, budget, and bake-time findings above.

## Conflicts

- **Source unit scale.** Valve code fixes 16 units per luxel but does not define the metre conversion. The two common Hammer conventions are 1 unit = 1 inch (40.6 cm per luxel) and 1 unit = 0.75 inch (30.5 cm per luxel); this report gives the range instead of choosing one. — Valve `bsplib.h` line 44; the exact official Hammer unit definition was not found in the SDK.
- **Unity's default lightmap resolution.** The Unity 6 `lightmapResolution` API page says "the default value is 40" and also "the default value is 2" in the same remarks block. The 40 figure matches the Lighting Settings default and is used here; the "2" sentence is treated as a documentation error. — Unity 6 Scripting API, `LightingSettings.lightmapResolution`, https://docs.unity.com/en-us/engine/6000.0/script-reference/unityengine/lightingsettings/lightmapresolution
- **"Noise-free" vs "deterministic."** This report uses "noise-free" to mean no random variance, not zero error. A fixed 128-ray gather is deterministic but still has quadrature error; classical radiosity is deterministic but still has patch/visibility discretization error. A path-traced bake is neither noise-free nor deterministic in the same sense. — Project constraint; Goral et al. 1984; Cohen et al. 1988; the harbagsa bake constants.
- **"Overshoot" is overloaded.** In progressive radiosity, "overshooting" usually means shooting more flux than the patch currently has to accelerate convergence, and it can create negative unshot flux; it is not the same thing as a blocky patch boundary. The visual artifact literature talks about blocky shadows, Mach bands, and unresolved discontinuities. — Gortler, Cohen & Slusallek 1994, DOI 10.1109/38.329094, abstract; Hanrahan CS348B Lecture 17, "Artifacts."
- **Source's 100-bounce default is a cap, not a count.** VRAD's `numbounce` defaults to 100, but `BounceLight` stops early when the added RGB is below 1.0, so a typical bake may use fewer bounces. The number is a ceiling with an energy-based exit. — Valve `src/utils/vrad/vrad.cpp`, lines 51 and 1717, https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/utils/vrad/vrad.cpp
- **Unreal's bounce guidance is a trade, not a fixed default.** Epic says bounce 1 dominates and successive bounces are nearly free but add little unless diffuse colours are near 1; indoor scenes may still need more bounces. The main Lightmass page does not state a single default bounce count, so this report does not attribute one. — Epic "Make Lightmass World Info Settings" (`Num Indirect Lighting Bounces`), https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/BlueprintAPI/Utilities/Struct/MakeLightmassWorldInfoSettings
- **idTech 7's "2x2 texels per square metre."** This is read as 2 texels per linear metre (0.5 m per texel), not 4 texels per linear metre. The slide's wording is compact; the 50 cm/texel reading is the one consistent with the storage and bake-time discussion. — Sousa, SIGGRAPH 2025, "Past: Global Illumination on idTech 7," https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2025/content/SOUSA_SIGGRAPH_2025_Final.pdf
- **Frostbite's 20-25 cm is a target, not a universal shipped value.** It is the *Battlefront II* target from the talk; other Frostbite titles and assets can differ. It is still the most directly comparable production figure for harbagsa's 25 cm. — O'Donnell, GDC 2018, "Lightmap density," https://media.contentapi.ea.com/content/dam/eacom/frostbite/files/gdc2018-precomputedgiobalilluminationinfrostbite.pdf
- **15.8% valid texels is not packing efficiency.** The valid count excludes gutters and invalid texels; the atlas is partly empty and partly padding. The derived 35-63% allocation estimate is an inference from the chart math, not a measured occupancy. — `src/bake/atlas.odin`; `docs/evidence/mixed-lighting.md` (`valid_texels 165,334`).
- **The 4-texel gutter is conservative, not incorrect.** A bilinear tap needs one texel of border; four is safe but wastes atlas on small charts. Calling it "over-engineered" means "more than the sampling footprint requires," not "a bug that causes wrong pixels." — `src/bake/bake.odin` (`GUTTER_TEXELS 4`); Unity's 2-texel padding and Frostbite's chart-mask packing show smaller margins are used in production.
- **One bounce is a design choice, not automatically a defect.** ADR 0045 says one bounce covers the corner/wall cases for the flat box arena; the production sources show one bounce is at the low end but not outside shipped practice. The quality question is whether the second-order colour bleeding is visible enough to justify the bake cost. — `docs/adr/0045-mixed-static-baked-indirect.md`; Valve/q3map2/Unity/Epic bounce sources above.
- **Path-traced bakers are context, not a recommendation.** Lightmass, Unity Progressive, Frostbite Flux, and idTech 7 are stochastic; they are cited for budgets, storage, and production lessons. The project constraint excludes them as a replacement for harbagsa's deterministic bake. — Project constraint; the systems' own descriptions (photons, path samples, path-traced lightmaps).
- **Enlighten is radiosity-based but not strictly noise-free.** The runtime solve is deterministic given the form factors, but the precompute computes visibility with rays (`Irradiance Quality`, default 8192 rays/pixel) and the docs warn of noise. It sits between the two families. — Unity "Introduction to Realtime Global Illumination using Enlighten," https://docs.unity3d.com/Manual/realtime-gi-using-enlighten.html; ARM guide, "Clustering and light transport," https://support.arm.com/documentation/100140/0201/global-illumination-in-unity-with-enlighten/the-structure-of-enlighten/pre-compute
- **RNM "triples" vs Source's "four."** RNM itself stores three directional lightmaps; Source's bump-lit face also keeps the base lightmap, so its per-face cost is four maps. Both statements are correct in their own scope; this report says "triples" for RNM and "base plus three" for Source. — Mitchell 2006, slide "Basis for Radiosity Normal Mapping"; Valve `lightmap.cpp` line 3027, https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/utils/vrad/lightmap.cpp#L3027
- **Source's 32/35 border cap is not a precise border spec.** The header says 32 luxels without border and 35 including border; the comment describes the extra as rounding allowance. The exact per-side border is not stated, so this report uses the cap only to show that Source bounds face charts, not to claim a specific gutter width. — Valve `src/public/bspfile.h` lines 30-35, https://github.com/ValveSoftware/source-sdk-2013/blob/master/src/public/bspfile.h#L30-L35
